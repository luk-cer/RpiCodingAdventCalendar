26Weather Station

The BME280 measures temperature, humidity and air pressure precisely. You'll write your own driver straight from the datasheet — reading registers and calibration data over I2C — and build a weather station on the OLED.

🧰You need

  • Pico W + breadboard
  • BME280 sensor board (I2C)
  • 0.96″ SSD1306 OLED
  • 8 jumper wires

🔍Two devices, one bus

Both the OLED and the BME280 connect to the same SDA and SCL wires. The OLED answers at address 0x3C, the BME280 at 0x76 (or 0x77, depending on the board). That's the beauty of I2C.

BME280 or BMP280? They look identical. The BMP280 has no humidity sensor. Step 2 checks which one you have. Both work for temperature and pressure.

🔌Wiring

Two wires into the same Pico pin's column is fine: everything in a breadboard column is connected. Your BME280 board may have 6 pins (VCC GND SCL SDA CSB SDO) — leave CSB and SDO unconnected.

📓Code, step by step

1. Scan the bus

2. Registers and the chip ID

I2C sensors are organised as numbered registers — little memory cells you read and write. The datasheet lists them. Register 0xD0 holds a chip ID: 0x60 for a BME280, 0x58 for a BMP280.

3. Calibration data

Every BME280 is measured at the factory, and correction values are stored inside it. The raw readings are meaningless without them. They live at 0x88–0xA1 and 0xE1–0xE7. struct.unpack turns bytes into numbers: < = little-endian, H = unsigned 16-bit, h = signed 16-bit.

4. Start measuring

Three configuration registers: humidity oversampling (0xF2, must be written first), temperature/pressure oversampling + mode (0xF4), and timing/filter (0xF5). 0x27 means "1× oversampling for both, normal mode" (measure continuously).

5. Raw readings

All results sit in 8 bytes from 0xF7: pressure (20 bits), temperature (20 bits), humidity (16 bits). We glue the bytes together with shifts.

6. Compensation formulas

These come straight from the BME280 datasheet (section 8.1). Temperature is computed first, because it produces t_fine, which the pressure and humidity formulas need. Don't worry about understanding every constant — this is what "implementing a datasheet" looks like.

7. Package it as a driver

Everything above, tidied into a class in its own file. Save this on the Pico as bme280_mini.py — then any program can import it.

8. The weather station

Big temperature on top, humidity and pressure below, and an altitude estimate from the barometric formula. Air pressure drops about 1 hPa per 8 m of height — so SEA_LEVEL_HPA must be today's sea-level pressure from a weather website for a good altitude.

Try it: carry the Pico (on a power bank) up or down a flight of stairs and watch the altitude change by a few metres. The BME280 is sensitive enough to notice!

🧠What you learned

  • Several devices on one I2C bus; registers, readfrom_mem, writeto_mem.
  • Reading a datasheet: chip ID, calibration constants, bit packing, compensation formulas.
  • struct.unpack, signed vs unsigned numbers, writing your own reusable driver module.
  • The barometric formula; scaling text with a frame buffer.

⭐Exercise: pressure trend and forecast

Falling air pressure often means bad weather is coming; rising pressure means it's clearing up. Turn your station into a forecaster:

  1. Store a pressure reading every 10 minutes (use 10 seconds while testing), keeping the last 36 (6 hours).
  2. Draw them as a small graph on the right side of the OLED, scaled between the minimum and maximum stored values.
  3. Compare the newest reading with the one 3 hours ago: rising by more than 1.5 hPa → "Clearing ☀", falling by more than 1.5 hPa → "Rain?", otherwise "Steady".
Hint

Keep the stored values in a list and draw them with oled.line() between neighbouring points. Guard against all values being equal (division by zero when scaling) by making the range at least 1 hPa.

Show solution

This is roughly how the "storm glass" barometers of the 1800s and today's simple weather stations predict the weather. It's right surprisingly often!
