10Digital Thermometer

The DS18B20 does all the maths itself and simply tells the Pico the temperature over a single data wire. You'll meet your first communication protocol, 1-Wire, and compare it with yesterday's thermistor.

🧰You need

  • Pico W + breadboard
  • KY-001 DS18B20 temperature sensor
  • KY-012 Active buzzer (from Day 5)
  • 8 jumper wires

🔍Meet the DS18B20

It looks like a transistor, but inside is a complete digital thermometer: accurate to ±0.5 °C, with up to 0.0625 °C resolution. It speaks 1-Wire: power, ground and one data line that carries messages in both directions. Every DS18B20 has a unique 64-bit serial number burnt in at the factory, so many of them can share the same wire. The KY-001 already has the required 4.7 kΩ pull-up resistor on board.

🔌Wiring

If the sensor gets hot to the touch, unplug immediately — it's connected backwards. Check the +/−/S labels on your module.

📓Code, step by step

1. Find sensors on the bus

MicroPython has the drivers built in. OneWire handles the low-level protocol on a pin, DS18X20 knows the thermometer's commands. scan() asks "who's there?" and returns a list of serial numbers (ROM codes).

The first byte 28 is the family code meaning "DS18B20". If you get Found 0, check the wiring.

2. Measure

Reading the temperature is two steps: tell all sensors to start converting, wait until they're done (up to 750 ms at full resolution), then read each one.

3. Live readings

Hold the sensor between your fingers and watch the plotter. Compare with the KY-013 yesterday: the DS18B20 reacts a little slower (its plastic package needs to warm up) but its readings are much steadier.

4. Resolution vs. speed

The DS18B20 can measure with 9, 10, 11 or 12 bits. Fewer bits = coarser steps but faster conversions. The setting lives in the sensor's scratchpad memory, byte 4. Let's see the difference:

9 bits gives 0.5 °C steps, 12 bits 0.0625 °C steps. The setting isn't saved permanently, so after a power cycle the sensor is back to 12 bits.

5. Too-hot alarm

A simple watchdog: if the temperature passes a limit, beep. Hold the sensor to test it (set LIMIT a degree or two above room temperature).

The program handles any number of sensors: plug in a second DS18B20 on the same data wire and it will appear automatically.

🧠What you learned

  • Digital sensors and the 1-Wire bus; unique ROM addresses.
  • onewire, ds18x20, scan(), convert_temp(), read_temp().
  • The trade-off between resolution and speed; writing configuration to a sensor.

⭐Exercise: a "fever thermometer" that knows when it's done

A medical thermometer beeps when the reading has stopped changing. Build one:

  1. Wait until someone grabs the sensor: the temperature rises at least 1 °C above the starting value. Print "Measuring…".
  2. Keep the last 6 readings. When the difference between the highest and lowest of them is at most 0.1 °C, the reading is stable: beep three times and print the result.
  3. Then wait until the sensor has cooled back below start + 0.5 °C before allowing a new measurement.
  4. Use 11-bit resolution to make it a bit quicker.
Hint

Structure it as a state machine with a variable state that is "idle", "measuring" or "cooling". Each loop iteration does one reading and decides whether to switch state.

Show solution

Each state only looks for the one condition that moves it to the next state. This pattern scales well: adding a "too cold to measure" state, for example, would just be another branch.

Body heat through fingers takes a minute or more to stabilise — real thermometers use clever prediction to finish sooner. Could you predict the final value from the shape of the curve?
