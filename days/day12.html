12Click! The Thermostat

Halfway there! Today the Pico switches something with a real mechanical relay — click! — and you'll build the same control logic as the thermostat on your radiator, including the trick that stops it clicking on and off a hundred times a minute.

🧰You need

  • Pico W + breadboard
  • KY-028 Digital temperature sensor (red board)
  • KY-019 Relay module
  • KY-034 7-colour LED (our pretend "heater")
  • 11 jumper wires

🔍Meet today's parts

KY-019 relay

An electromagnet that pulls a metal switch. A tiny signal from the Pico switches a completely separate circuit connected to the screw terminals: COM (common) is connected to NC (normally closed) when off, and to NO (normally open) when the relay is on.

KY-028 temperature sensor

An NTC thermistor (like Day 9) plus a comparator chip. AO gives the analog divider voltage; DO switches between 0 and 1 when the temperature crosses a threshold you set with the blue potentiometer.

Low voltage only! The relay is rated for mains voltage, but never connect it to anything plugged into a wall socket. In this calendar it only ever switches 3.3 V.

The relay coil needs 5 V, so its + goes to VBUS (pin 40), not the 3.3 V rail. Its signal pin is safe for the Pico. Only VBUS — never connect 5 V to a GPIO.

🔌Wiring

Follow the current: 3.3 V from the rail goes into COM; when the relay clicks on, it comes out of NO and powers the 7-colour LED. The Pico never touches the LED directly — that's the point of a relay.

📓Code, step by step

1. Click!

You should hear the click and see the little red LED on the relay board. If the relay is on when the pin is off, you have an "active-low" version: swap on() and off() throughout.

2. Temperature from AO

Same divider maths as Day 9. Warm the thermistor with your fingers to check the direction, and set NTC_ON_TOP accordingly.

On the KY-028 the analog value often goes down as it warms — the opposite of many KY-013 boards. If the temperature moves the wrong way when you warm it, flip NTC_ON_TOP.

3. The naive thermostat (and its problem)

"Heat if colder than 25 °C" sounds simple:

Hold the sensor so it sits right at 25 °C. Noise makes the reading wobble around the target and the relay chatters — click-clack-click. Real heaters, fridges and pumps would wear out quickly.

4. Hysteresis

The fix is a dead band: switch on below 24.5 °C, switch off above 25.5 °C, and in between keep doing whatever you were doing. That memory is called hysteresis.

5. Hardware backup: the comparator

The KY-028's DO pin is a thermostat in hardware! Turn the blue potentiometer slowly until the small LED on the board just switches; now DO changes when the temperature crosses that point, with no software involved. We'll print it alongside.

Set TARGET about 2 °C above room temperature. Pinch the sensor — the "heater" switches off once you've warmed it past the band; let go and it clicks back on as it cools.

🧠What you learned

  • Relays: COM/NO/NC, coil vs contacts, galvanic separation.
  • Comparators: a threshold decision made in hardware.
  • Hysteresis — the most important idea in on/off control.

⭐Exercise: a thermostat that protects its hardware

  1. Minimum run times: once switched on, the heater must stay on for at least 5 s; once off, it must stay off for at least 5 s — no matter what the temperature does. (Real compressors need this.)
  2. Safety cut-off: use the comparator DO pin as an independent over-temperature alarm. Set the potentiometer to trigger a little above TARGET. When it fires, switch off immediately (ignoring the minimum on-time) and blink the on-board LED until it's cool again.
  3. Print the reason for every switch, e.g. ON (23.9 °C below band), OFF (safety cut-off!).
Hint

Record last_switch = ticks_ms() whenever the relay changes. The thermostat proposes a new state; you only accept it if enough time has passed. Find out which DO level means "too hot" by warming the sensor and watching the module's LED.

Show solution

The logic is layered: safety first (it overrides everything), then the minimum-time rule, then normal hysteresis control. Keeping each rule in its own clearly ordered if makes it easy to reason about.
