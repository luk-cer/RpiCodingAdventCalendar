19Clap Your Hands

Two microphone modules turn the Pico into a sound-level meter for carol singing. You'll sample a fast-changing signal, measure its loudness, show it on the traffic light — and build a clap switch.

🧰You need

  • Pico W + breadboard
  • KY-037 Big microphone module
  • KY-038 Small microphone module
  • LED Traffic Light module
  • 13 jumper wires

🔍How the microphone modules work

Each has an electret microphone (the silver can), an amplifier/comparator chip and a potentiometer. AO outputs the sound wave itself: a voltage that wiggles up and down around a middle value hundreds or thousands of times a second. DO goes high when the sound is louder than the threshold set by the potentiometer. The big KY-037 is more sensitive than the small KY-038.

Adjust each potentiometer (many turns!) until the module's DO LED is just off in a quiet room. A clap should then make it flash.

1. One reading means nothing

You get roughly the same middle value even while talking. That's because one sample is just one point on a fast wave — it could be the top, bottom or middle. To measure loudness we need many samples quickly and look at how far they spread.

2. Peak-to-peak loudness

We grab 300 samples as fast as possible (about 10 ms of sound) and take the difference between the highest and lowest value. Silence = small spread, loud = large spread.

Compare the two microphones: the KY-037's line should jump higher for the same sound.

3. A smoother level

Speech is choppy. A "fast attack, slow release" filter rises immediately with loud sounds but falls slowly — like the bouncing bars on a music player.

4. Traffic-light VU meter

Green for normal, green + yellow for loud, all three for very loud. Find good thresholds by watching the numbers while you sing.

5. Counting claps with DO

The digital outputs do the threshold work in hardware. An interrupt counts claps on the big microphone; a clap makes DO flicker for a few ms, so we ignore edges within 150 ms of the last one.

🧠What you learned

  • Sound is a fast wave — single samples don't tell you loudness, the spread of many does.
  • Peak-to-peak measurement; attack/release smoothing.
  • Hardware thresholds (DO) vs software thresholds (AO).

⭐Exercise: double-clap Christmas tree switch

The famous "clap on, clap off" gadget — but single claps (or a door slamming) must not trigger it.

  1. Toggle the on-board LED (our Christmas tree) only on a double clap: two claps with a gap between 0.2 and 0.8 seconds.
  2. Three or more quick claps in a row should not count as a double clap.
  3. Show the tree's state on the traffic light too: green = tree on, red = tree off.
Hint

Store clap timestamps in the interrupt. In the main loop, wait until 0.8 s have passed after the last clap (the "sequence" is finished), then look at how many claps there were and the gap between them.

Show solution

Waiting until the sequence is over before deciding is the key: only then do we know whether it was 1, 2 or more claps. That costs 0.8 s of delay, which is why real clap switches feel slightly sluggish.
