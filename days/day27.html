27Digital Snow Globe

The MPU6050 feels gravity and rotation — the same kind of sensor that turns your phone's screen. Today it becomes a spirit level, and in the exercise a snow globe where the flakes really fall "down" however you hold the breadboard.

🧰You need

  • Pico W + breadboard
  • MPU6050 board (GY-521)
  • 0.96″ SSD1306 OLED
  • 8 jumper wires

🔍Accelerometer + gyroscope

Inside the chip are microscopic structures etched in silicon (MEMS). The accelerometer has tiny masses on springs: gravity and movement push them, and the chip measures how far. At rest it measures only gravity — 1 g pointing down — which tells you how the sensor is tilted. The gyroscope measures how fast it rotates, in degrees per second. Both report three axes: X, Y and Z (printed on the board).

🔌Wiring

XDA, XCL, AD0 and INT stay unconnected. (AD0 selects the address: unconnected/low = 0x68, high = 0x69.)

📓Code, step by step

1. Wake it up

The MPU6050 starts in sleep mode. Writing 0 to the power management register 0x6B wakes it. Register 0x75 ("WHO_AM_I") should answer 0x68.

Some clone boards answer 0x70 or 0x72 to WHO_AM_I. They're compatible chips and work with this code.

2. Read all sensors at once

14 bytes starting at 0x3B: accelerometer X, Y, Z, temperature, gyroscope X, Y, Z — each a signed 16-bit number, high byte first (big-endian, hence > in the format string). With the default ranges, 16384 = 1 g and 131 = 1 °/s.

Lying flat, Z should read about +1 g and X, Y about 0. Tilt the breadboard and watch gravity "move" between the axes. Rotate it quickly to see the gyro values jump.

3. Tilt angles

Gravity's direction gives the tilt. Roll (tilting left/right around X) and pitch (tilting forward/back around Y) come from atan2, like the joystick angle on Day 20.

4. A spirit level on the OLED

Like the bubble in a builder's level, our "bubble" moves away from the low side. A smoothing filter keeps it from jittering. When the board is level within 1°, the bubble turns into a filled circle.

If the bubble moves the wrong way on your board, flip a sign: 64 - int(...) instead of 64 + int(...). It depends on how the module sits relative to the screen.

🧠What you learned

  • MEMS accelerometers and gyroscopes; gravity as a tilt reference.
  • Big-endian vs little-endian (compare with Day 26's BME280!), scaling raw values to physical units.
  • Roll and pitch with atan2; drawing circles from x² + y² = r².

⭐Exercise: the snow globe

  1. 40 snowflakes, each with a position and a velocity. Every frame, gravity (the accelerometer's X and Y) accelerates them, and a little air resistance slows them down.
  2. Flakes stop at the screen edges (and lose speed) — so they pile up on whichever side is "down". Tilt the breadboard and watch them slide!
  3. Shake it: if the total acceleration differs from 1 g by more than 0.6 g, give every flake a random kick.
  4. Bonus: draw a tiny house or tree in the middle that the snow falls around (just draw it — no collision needed).
Hint

Per frame: vx += gx * k; vy += gy * k; vx *= 0.96; x += vx. Total acceleration is sqrt(ax² + ay² + az²). Which accelerometer axis maps to screen x and y depends on how your boards are mounted — try it and swap/negate as needed.

Show solution

This is a tiny physics simulation: each frame applies forces to velocities and velocities to positions (Euler integration). Storing positions as floats keeps motion smooth even though pixels are whole numbers.

Want bigger flakes? Draw a tiny plus sign (5 pixels) instead of a single pixel. Want more flakes? Watch the frame rate — at some point the Pico spends all its time drawing.
