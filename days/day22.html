22Infrared Remote

Every TV remote sends invisible light pulses in a secret code. Today you'll capture those pulses, decode the most common protocol (NEC) yourself, and make the Pico send codes with its own IR LED.

🧰You need

  • Pico W + breadboard
  • KY-022 IR receiver
  • KY-005 IR transmitter
  • 1 × 220 Ω resistor
  • Any TV / media remote control
  • 7 jumper wires

🔍How IR remotes work

The remote's IR LED doesn't simply switch on and off — when "on", it flickers at 38 000 times per second (the carrier). The receiver (a VS1838B-type chip on the KY-022) only reacts to light flickering at exactly that rate, so sunlight and lamps don't confuse it. Its output is the "envelope": LOW while the carrier is present, HIGH otherwise.

The NEC protocol (used by many cheap remotes) sends: a 9 ms burst, a 4.5 ms pause, then 32 bits. Each bit is a 562 µs burst followed by a pause: short (562 µs) = 0, long (1687 µs) = 1. The 32 bits are: address, inverted address, command, inverted command — the inverted copies let the receiver detect errors.

🔌Wiring

The KY-005's LED has no resistor on most boards, so we add 220 Ω (the range will be about a metre — plenty for testing).

📓Code, step by step

1. Capture the pulses

An interrupt records the time of every edge. When no edge has arrived for 20 ms, the message is complete and we convert timestamps into durations.

9 ms, 4.5 ms, then pairs of ~560 and ~560/~1690 — that's NEC! (67 = 1 leader pair + 32 bit pairs + 1 final burst.) Other remotes (Sony, RC5, Samsung…) show different patterns.

2. Decode NEC

We check the leader, then read 32 spaces: long = 1. NEC sends the least significant bit first, so bit i goes to position i.

Press different buttons and write down their command numbers. (Some remotes use "extended NEC" where address and inverted address don't match — that's why we only check the command.)

3. Make the Pico a transmitter

To send, we need a 38 kHz carrier — PWM with freq(38000) and about ⅓ duty cycle. Switching the duty between ⅓ and 0 creates bursts and pauses. Timing matters, so we use absolute deadlines with ticks_us rather than sleep_us (which would add up small errors).

🧠What you learned

  • Carrier modulation (38 kHz) and why it makes IR robust.
  • Decoding a real protocol from pulse timings; LSB-first bit packing with |=, <<, >>, &, ^.
  • Precise timing with absolute deadlines.

⭐Exercise: learning universal remote

Many remotes don't use NEC. But we don't need to understand a protocol to copy it — just record the raw durations and play them back.

  1. Add a learn mode: the program asks you to press a button on your remote, records the raw durations and stores them under a name you type (e.g. "volume_up").
  2. Save all learned codes to remote.json so they survive a restart.
  3. Add a send mode: type a name and the Pico transmits it. Test it on your real TV (point the IR LED at it, closer than 1 m)!
  4. Ignore captures with fewer than 6 durations (noise).
Hint

A dictionary {name: [durations]} converts straight to JSON. Turn off the receiver's interrupt while transmitting (rx.irq(handler=None)) so the Pico doesn't record its own signal, then re-enable it.

Show solution

Some TVs expect the code twice, or need a repeat frame to react. If yours ignores a single send, try send_raw(d); time.sleep_ms(40); send_raw(d).
