14Magnet Detective

Reed switches only say yes or no. Hall-effect sensors can measure a magnetic field's strength and even tell the north pole from the south. Three of them today — and a magnetometer you can read in the shell.

🧰You need

  • Pico W + breadboard
  • KY-003 Digital Hall sensor
  • KY-024 Linear Hall sensor (red board)
  • KY-035 Analog Hall sensor
  • A magnet
  • 11 jumper wires

🔍The Hall effect

When current flows through a thin semiconductor plate and a magnetic field passes through it, the electrons get pushed to one side, creating a tiny voltage across the plate. That's the Hall effect (Edwin Hall, 1879). The sensor chip amplifies it.

ModuleChipOutput
KY-003A3144 (switch)Digital: snaps to 0 when a (south) pole comes close.
KY-03549E (linear)Analog: half of the supply voltage with no field, higher for one pole, lower for the other.
KY-02449E + comparatorBoth: AO like the KY-035, DO with an adjustable threshold.

🔌Wiring

📓Code, step by step

1. The digital sensor

Bring each side of your magnet to the sensor's flat face. The A3144 only reacts to one pole — that's how you can find which side of a magnet is south!

2. Analog field strength

With no magnet nearby, a linear Hall sensor outputs about half the supply voltage. Bringing a pole closer pushes it up or down. Run this with the plotter open and wave the magnet around both analog sensors.

3. Calibrate the zero point

"Half the supply" is never exactly 32768. We measure the true zero-field value at start-up (magnet far away) and subtract it. Positive and negative numbers now tell the two poles apart.

4. Convert to millitesla

The 49E chip's sensitivity is about 1.4 mV per gauss at 5 V and scales with supply voltage, so at 3.3 V it's roughly 0.92 mV/G = 9.2 mV per millitesla (mT). That's approximate — but good enough to compare magnets. (Earth's field is about 0.05 mT; a fridge magnet up close is 5–10 mT.)

5. A shell magnetometer

A text bar graph: the bar grows to the right for one pole and to the left for the other. "#" * n repeats a character n times.

Which sign means north depends on which side of the sensor faces the magnet. Use the KY-003 from step 1 (it reacts to south) to find out, then fix the labels in the code.

🧠What you learned

  • The Hall effect; switching vs linear Hall sensors; magnetic poles.
  • Zero-point calibration and unit conversion from datasheet sensitivity.
  • Building text visualisations with string repetition.

⭐Exercise: rev counter

Hall sensors count rotations in bike computers, car engines and wind gauges. Tape the magnet to something that spins — a bike wheel, a fidget spinner, a pencil you twirl, a desk fan (switched off and turned by hand!) — and measure its speed.

  1. Use an interrupt on the KY-003 (falling edge) to record the time of each pass in microseconds (time.ticks_us()).
  2. Calculate RPM (revolutions per minute) from the time between the last two passes. Print it twice a second.
  3. If no pass has happened for 2 seconds, report 0 RPM.
  4. Bonus: if you know the wheel circumference, print the speed in km/h.
Hint

In the handler, store period = ticks_diff(now, last) and last = now. RPM = 60 000 000 / period (µs). Ignore periods shorter than ~5 ms (bounce or a too-strong magnet).

Show solution

The interrupt handler measures the period precisely; the main loop only displays it. Using microseconds gives good resolution even for fast rotations.

With one magnet you get one pulse per revolution. Two magnets on opposite sides double the resolution — remember to divide by two!
