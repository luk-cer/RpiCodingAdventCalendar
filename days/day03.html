3Two-Colour Glow

A pin can only be on or off… or can it? Today you'll fake "half on" with PWM to fade LEDs smoothly and mix red and green into orange and yellow, using the two bi-colour LED modules.

🧰You need

  • Pico W + breadboard
  • KY-011 Bi-colour LED 5 mm
  • KY-029 Bi-colour LED 3 mm
  • 4 × 220 Ω resistors
  • 10 jumper wires

🔌Wiring

Each module holds one LED package with a red and a green chip that share the − (cathode) leg. These modules have no current-limiting resistors, so every colour gets its own 220 Ω resistor in the top half of the breadboard. Each resistor connects a Pico pin (left leg) to an LED pin (right leg, via a wire down to the module).

Check the labels on your boards! Some KY-011/KY-029 boards are marked R, G/Y and − in a different order. Connect the − pin to ground and the other two through resistors. If a colour comes out swapped, just swap the pin numbers in the code.

📓Code, step by step

1. Plain on/off first

Before anything fancy, check every colour lights up. This is just Day 1 again.

2. What is PWM?

Pulse Width Modulation switches a pin on and off very fast — here 1000 times per second. Your eye can't see the flicker and just sees the average. If the pin is on 25 % of each cycle (the duty cycle), the LED looks dimmer. In MicroPython the duty cycle is a number from 0 (always off) to 65535 (always on).

3. A smooth fade

Changing the duty cycle step by step fades the LED. But our eyes are not linear: going from 0 → 10 % looks like a big jump, 90 → 100 % hardly changes anything. Squaring the brightness (a simple gamma correction) makes the fade look even.

🧠What you learned

  • PWM(Pin(n)), .freq(), .duty_u16(0…65535) dim an output.
  • Gamma correction (x * x) makes brightness changes look natural.
  • Mixing two colours of light creates new ones (additive colour mixing).
  • LEDs without built-in resistors always need a series resistor.

⭐Exercise: two flickering candles

  1. Turn each module into a candle: a warm orange that flickers randomly. Use the random module (random.random() gives a float between 0 and 1).
  2. The flicker should look natural: brightness changes should be small most of the time, with an occasional bigger dip. Each candle flickers independently.
  3. Bonus: every ~5 seconds one candle "gutters" — dims almost to off for half a second and recovers.
Hint

Keep a brightness per candle. Each step, move it a little toward a random target (e.g. b += (target - b) * 0.3) and pick a new target often. Keep the green part at about 25–35 % of the red part for an orange colour.

Show solution

Each candle is a small dictionary holding its current brightness and its target. Smoothing towards the target ("exponential smoothing") turns jumpy random numbers into a natural-looking wobble. Occasional larger dips come from rarely choosing a much lower target.

The "gutter" recovers by itself, because the smoothing pulls brightness back towards the normal target range.
