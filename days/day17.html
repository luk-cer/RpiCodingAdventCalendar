17Advent Candle Watch

The flame sensor sees the infrared glow of a fire. Today you'll build a watchdog for the Advent candle: it notices when the candle is lit, times how long it burns, and reminds you before it's left alone too long.

🧰You need

  • Pico W + breadboard
  • KY-026 Flame sensor (red board)
  • KY-012 Active buzzer
  • A tea-light candle, matches — and an adult
  • 8 jumper wires

Fire safety: an adult lights and watches the candle. Keep it on a plate, at least 20 cm from the breadboard and wires, and never leave it unattended. This project is a reminder, not a safety device — never rely on it.

🔍How it sees fire

The black LED-like part is an infrared phototransistor, most sensitive to 760–1100 nm — the wavelengths flames emit strongly. AO gives an analog value (how much IR); DO flips when it passes the potentiometer's threshold. Sunlight and halogen lamps contain lots of IR too, so expect false alarms near windows.

1. Look at the raw signal

Run this with the plotter open. After a few seconds, have the adult light the candle about 20–30 cm in front of the sensor.

On most KY-026 boards the value drops when there's a flame (more IR → the phototransistor conducts more). Notice the wobble — that's the flame flickering!

2. Baseline and difference

Every room has different background IR. Instead of fixed numbers, we measure a baseline without flame at start-up and look at the change. abs() makes it work whichever direction your board goes.

3. Lit or not — with hysteresis

A flickering flame produces a wobbly signal, so we use two thresholds (Day 12's hysteresis again) to avoid "lit / not lit / lit" chatter.

4. Burn timer with reminders

We remember when the candle was lit. After REMIND_AFTER seconds it beeps, and again every 30 s, until the candle is blown out. For testing it's 60 s; for real use set it to 30 minutes.

⭐Exercise: real flame or just a lamp?

Point a torch or an incandescent bulb at the sensor — it probably thinks that's a candle too. But real flames flicker (typically 5–15 times per second), while lamps glow steadily. Teach the Pico the difference:

  1. Sample AO 200 times per second for 2 seconds (400 samples).
  2. Calculate the mean and the standard deviation (how much the values wobble around the mean).
  3. Also count how often the signal crosses the mean — a measure of how fast it wobbles.
  4. Decide: strong signal + high wobble = "flame 🔥", strong but steady = "lamp 💡", weak = "nothing". Print the numbers so you can tune your thresholds.
Hint

Standard deviation: sqrt(sum((x - mean)**2 for x in samples) / len(samples)). A mean-crossing happens when one sample is above the mean and the next one below (or vice versa). Use time.sleep_us(5000) between samples.

This is a tiny bit of signal processing: we reduce 400 numbers to three features (strength, wobble, crossings) and classify with simple rules. The thresholds below are starting points — print the features for each light source and adjust.
